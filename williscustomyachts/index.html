+Why most savings pitches fail

Most energy vendors carry one product, so every facility gets the same answer. The case study they show you is a different building's prescription. And the people who know your equipment best, your electrical and mechanical service companies, are paid for service hours, not for kilowatt-hours you never use. Nobody in that model is paid to measure your facility first, so the waste survives the upgrade.

That is the entire difference here: the measurement comes first, on your own bills and your own meters, and the fix is chosen to match what it finds.

A third of the bill is set in thirty minutes.

FPL bills demand at $13.41/kW on the single highest 30-minute pull each month.14 October billed 723 kW. January billed 421 kW. Same buildings, same equipment: the gap is schedule, not necessity.

About that solar quote.

Willis already has an array proposal on the table. Good instinct. Wrong order. Every kilowatt hour the yard stops wasting is a panel you never have to buy, and the same battery that shaves demand peaks also shifts solar. Efficiency first, storage second, panels last: the array that survives that math is smaller, carries less roof load, and earns the same federal credits.3

Bring the quote to the walkthrough. We will price the same bill impact three ways against the yard's actual load shape from the interval data: array alone, storage alone, and both sized together. And in Stuart, the battery is also the thing that keeps security, pumps, and in-progress builds alive when the grid blinks in September.

The pumps never stop.

Boats come out of the water here and the water keeps moving through them, hull after hull, around the clock. A motor that never rests bills every hour of the year.

What that costs: one always-on kilowatt is about $954 a year at today's rates.1 A yard this size runs hundreds of them. The fix is unglamorous and proven: count them, right size them, slow the ones that do not need full flow, and let the meter show the difference.

Uneven power is quietly aging your best equipment.

Motors, welders, and compressors do not draw power smoothly. Hard starts and uneven draw push distortion back through the panels, and distortion becomes heat inside windings and compressor circuits. Heat is what retires equipment early.12

On this yard the most expensive machines are the business itself: the 155 ton lift, the CNC shop, the compressors. Cleaning up how power reaches them is less an energy measure than an equipment life measure, and it is measured, not guessed: we put instruments on the panels during the walkthrough and read the distortion directly.

Even in January, the meters never rest.

At 2 a.m. the office is dark and the lot is empty. The yard isn't. Pumps keep circulating water through hulls in dry storage, security and lighting hold, and compressed air never switches off. January, with no cooling to speak of, still cost $20,174: an average draw of roughly 250 kW, around the clock.14

79percent of the annual usage at this address does not follow the seasons. The seasons barely move this bill. What runs it is the equipment that works year round, and the load factor on the big meters says a large share of it never switches off at night.14

This is why we start with the meter, not the roof: on a property whose meters never come close to resting, the fix attached to the load that never stops is the one that pays the most.

Your worst meter is hiding in the average.

Nobody ranks them, because ranking them means pulling twelve months of interval data from every meter, and it is nobody's job to go get it. It is the first thing we do, before a single piece of equipment is discussed, and the same ranking scales cleanly to the new shop once it's built.
